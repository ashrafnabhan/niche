# Uterine Niche (Cesarean Scar Defect, Isthmocele): State of the Art Review

*Causes, diagnosis and management. Prepared 10 November 2026.*

> **Note on sources.** Statements marked with a reference number were checked against PubMed abstracts retrieved for this review (reference list at the end, with DOIs). Statements without a number reflect widely cited background knowledge and the author's synthesis. Verify these against the primary papers before citing them in a manuscript or guideline. Full texts were not reviewed, so effect sizes come from abstracts.

---

## 1. Key messages

- A **niche** is a sonographic finding, and a **cesarean scar disorder (CSDi)** is a clinical condition. The 2023 Delphi consensus separates the two: CSDi is a niche plus defined symptoms [5].
- Niches are very common. They appear in roughly half or more of women after a cesarean section (CS) [6,7]. Most are asymptomatic.
- Symptomatic niches mainly cause **postmenstrual spotting** and **pain with menses**. They are also associated with **subfertility** and with serious obstetric risks in later pregnancies (cesarean scar pregnancy, placenta accreta spectrum, scar dehiscence or rupture).
- The best-established prevention is at the time of CS. Technique matters, but **single- versus double-layer closure remains unsettled** because RCT and meta-analytic findings are inconsistent [8,9,10]. **Purse-string (double-layer) closure** looks promising, with moderate-certainty evidence for fewer defects [11].
- For treatment, **no high-quality RCT** establishes the best option. Hysteroscopic resection, laparoscopic or robotic repair, vaginal repair and **LNG-IUS** are all used. The choice is driven by residual myometrial thickness (RMT), symptoms, and whether the woman wants a pregnancy [12–14].

---

## 2. Terminology and definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Niche / CS scar defect / isthmocele | Indentation or discontinuity of the myometrium at the CS scar. The ISUOG-endorsed European Delphi defines it as an indentation of **at least 2 mm depth** on ultrasound [7]. |
| Cesarean scar disorder (CSDi) | A niche **plus at least 1 primary or 2 secondary symptoms**, after exclusion of other causes [5]. |
| Primary symptoms [5] | Postmenstrual spotting; pain during uterine bleeding; technical difficulty with catheter insertion at embryo transfer; secondary unexplained infertility with intrauterine fluid. |
| Secondary symptoms [5] | Dyspareunia; abnormal discharge; chronic pelvic pain; avoiding intercourse; odor with abnormal blood loss; secondary infertility despite ART; negative self-image; discomfort during leisure activities. |
| Cesarean scar pregnancy (CSP) | A distinct early-pregnancy diagnosis. A separate Delphi classification grades it by sac position and depth [15,16]. |

**Why this matters.** Before 2023, the literature mixed "niche" (an imaging finding) with "symptomatic niche". This inflated prevalence estimates of "disease" and made trials hard to compare. New studies should report niche presence and CSDi separately.

---

## 3. Epidemiology

- Reported niche prevalence after CS ranges widely, from about 24% to 70% or more. The range depends on imaging method (saline-contrast sonohysterography detects more than plain TVUS), the size threshold used, and the timing of assessment.
- Reviews and the recent Delphi work cite figures of about 50–60% of women with a prior CS [5,6].
- Prevalence is higher after repeat cesareans and is partly determined by surgical technique (section 4).
- The association with symptoms is real but not universal. In a meta-analysis, women with a scar defect had about **3.5 times the risk of abnormal uterine bleeding** (RR 3.47, 95% CI 2.02–5.97). AUB prevalence in unselected women with a defect was about **25%**. It was about **76%** in women referred for imaging for a gynecologic complaint [17]. This difference reflects referral bias and shows that a niche alone is often not the cause of symptoms.

## 4. Causes and risk factors

### 4.1 Pathophysiology
A niche is a **healing failure** of the uterine incision. The proposed mechanisms are:

1. **Poor tissue apposition and ischemia.** Tight or locked sutures, mass ligation and excessive tension reduce perfusion and impair healing.
2. **Incision location.** Low incisions (at or below the cervico-isthmic junction) heal into cervical tissue with more fibrous content and less muscle. Cervical glands in the incision may contribute.
3. **Scar position and anatomy.** A retroflexed uterus puts more tension on the scar and is associated with larger defects.
4. **Inflammation and impaired wound healing.** Patient factors and infection play a part.
5. **Mechanical factors after surgery.** Menstrual blood and mucus pool in the defect and drain slowly. This causes postmenstrual spotting, intrauterine fluid, inflammation and possibly a local microbial change [18].

### 4.2 Risk factors (summary of the literature)
| Domain | Factors |
|---|---|
| Surgical | Single-layer closure (inconsistent across trials), locked sutures, low or cervical incision, advanced cervical dilatation or labor at CS, incomplete or poor closure technique, inclusion of the decidua versus endometrial-sparing technique, technique and operator experience [9,19] |
| Obstetric | Number of prior CSs (dose-response), CS in labor, prolonged labor |
| Anatomical | Uterine retroflexion, adhesions between scar and abdominal wall |
| Maternal | Obesity, diabetes, smoking, conditions impairing wound healing |

A 2026 systematic review of surgery-related predisposing factors concluded that optimizing hysterotomy technique may reduce defect rates and their complications, and recommended that all women having a CS be informed about the risk [19].

### 4.3 Prevention at cesarean section
- **Single- vs double-layer closure.**
  - **2Close RCT** (n = 2292, Netherlands, first CS): double-layer closure did *not* reduce postmenstrual spotting (adjusted mean difference −0.07 days, 95% CI −0.37 to 0.22). It added about 4 minutes of operating time and was associated with *higher* niche prevalence (+4.7%, 95% CI 0.7–8.7) [8].
  - **2025 meta-analysis** (11 RCTs, 6058 participants): single-layer closure had a lower niche incidence at 6 months (risk difference −0.11) but with very high heterogeneity (I² = 91%) [9]. This heterogeneity (different suture materials, locking, endometrium-sparing technique, imaging protocols) limits confidence.
  - Net reading: **there is no reliable evidence that routine double-layer closure prevents niches**, and some data suggest that it does not.
- **Purse-string uterine suturing.** In a meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (751 participants), the relative risk of a scar defect was 0.45 (95% CI 0.36–0.58) with no heterogeneity for that outcome. The authors rated certainty as moderate for defect rate and low or very low for other ultrasound and surgical outcomes [11].
- **Other technique points commonly advised** (supported mostly by observational data and expert opinion): avoid very low hysterotomy, avoid locking and strangulating sutures, incorporate the full thickness of myometrium with good apposition of the edges, and consider incision placement relative to the isthmus.

## 5. Clinical presentation

**Gynecologic**
- Postmenstrual spotting (the hallmark). Mean menstrual duration in symptomatic women is prolonged, with brown discharge at the end of menses [17].
- Dysmenorrhea, chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia.
- Abnormal discharge.

**Reproductive**
- Secondary subfertility, intrauterine fluid, difficult embryo transfer. A systematic review of retrospective cohorts (1083 women with and 3987 without a niche) found a lower live birth rate after assisted reproduction (pooled aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.48–0.69), rated as low-grade evidence [18]. A 2026 review again emphasized a link with secondary infertility but concluded that causality is not proven [20].

**Obstetric (future pregnancy)**
- Cesarean scar pregnancy, placenta accreta spectrum, scar dehiscence, uterine rupture. The risk rises as RMT falls.

## 6. Diagnosis

### 6.1 First-line: transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS)
Standardized reporting follows the European Niche Taskforce Delphi [7].

**Timing.** Evaluation at least several months after CS is standard, because early images overstate niche prevalence. Assessment in the **early follicular phase / just after menses** (when fluid is present in the defect) improves visibility.

**Essential measurements (sagittal plane unless stated) [7]:**
- Niche **length** and **depth**
- **Residual myometrial thickness (RMT)** and **adjacent myometrial thickness (AMT)**
- Niche **width** (transverse plane)
- Presence of **branches**, with additional measurements for them
- Position relative to the cervical canal and internal os

**Enhancement.** Gel or saline contrast sonohysterography improves detection and measurement accuracy [7].

**Other points.** Report the uterine position, the number of niches, and the distance from the niche to the internal os. The RMT/AMT ratio is commonly used for grading ("large niche" is often defined as RMT <3 mm or loss of more than 50% of the adjacent myometrium).

### 6.2 Hysteroscopy
- Allows direct evaluation of the defect, the endometrium and associated findings (polyps, crypts, cysts, fibrotic tissue, blood, mucus, a dynamic valve effect, placental remnants).
- An ESGE Delphi proposed a standard: hysteroscopic definition of a niche as any indentation at the CS site, **combined with ultrasound** for RMT, and sub-classification as **simple, simple with one branch, or complex** [21].
- Hysteroscopy is a diagnostic complement and a treatment platform, not a replacement for ultrasound.

### 6.3 MRI
- Reserved for complex cases: very thin RMT, suspected extra-uterine extension, pre-operative planning for laparoscopic or vaginal repair, or when ultrasound is not feasible.

### 6.4 Diagnostic algorithm (proposed synthesis)
1. Symptomatic woman with prior CS → TVUS (preferably timed after menses; consider contrast sonography).
2. Niche found → apply the CSDi criteria. **Exclude other causes** of spotting, pain or infertility (infection, polyps, adenomyosis, endometriosis, hormonal or anatomical causes) [5].
3. Document RMT, AMT, niche dimensions and branching → plan treatment.
4. Hysteroscopy when treating, or when the diagnosis is uncertain.
5. Asymptomatic niche: **no treatment**; counsel on obstetric risk, and offer early first-trimester TVUS to locate the next pregnancy.

## 7. Management

### 7.1 Principles
- **Treat the symptomatic condition (CSDi), not the ultrasound finding.**
- Choose by symptom, fertility wish and RMT.
- Evidence is mostly from **case series and cohorts**. Randomized comparisons are few, and outcomes are not standardized, so treat success rates as approximate.

### 7.2 Conservative / medical
| Option | Evidence and role |
|---|---|
| Expectant | Appropriate for asymptomatic niches. |
| Combined oral contraceptive pill | Commonly used. Evidence is limited. May reduce spotting while taken. |
| **LNG-IUS** | A 2025 meta-analysis (3 studies, 344 women) found that LNG-IUS gave fewer total bleeding days and fewer spotting days than hysteroscopic resection at 6 months, and superior effectiveness at 12 months (OR 3.46, 95% CI 1.53–7.80). About 50% were amenorrheic at 1 year. The data come from few, mostly non-randomized studies, and apply to women **not wishing pregnancy** [22]. |
| Tranexamic acid, NSAIDs | Symptomatic relief only. |

### 7.3 Surgical
**Hysteroscopic niche resection (remodeling)**
- Resects the distal ("lower") edge and the fibrotic tissue and coagulates the defect, so menstrual blood can drain.
- Reported relief of AUB in **60–100%**. Conception after treatment in **25–100%** of women with infertility during follow-up. No major complications were reported in the systematic review of 27 studies [12].
- Best suited to **RMT of about ≥3 mm** (commonly used threshold; protect against perforation and thermal injury).
- It does **not** thicken the myometrium and does not restore the structural integrity of the scar. It is therefore not an option for those whose primary concern is the obstetric risk of a very thin scar.

**Laparoscopic / robotic repair (often combined with hysteroscopy)**
- Excision of the defect and layered closure restores myometrial thickness.
- Symptom relief in **78–94%**. Pregnancy after repair in about **37.5–90%** of women who desired it [13].
- Preferred when RMT is **thin (<2–3 mm)**, when pregnancy is planned and scar integrity matters, and with large niches.
- Hysteroscopic guidance (light transillumination) helps identify the defect. Combined approaches are common [13].

**Vaginal repair**
- An option for low niches accessible via the vagina. Evidence is limited and mostly retrospective.

**Hysterectomy**
- Definitive option for women who have completed childbearing and failed or declined other treatments.

**Repair in a subsequent pregnancy?** Pre-conception repair is the norm. Repair is not generally performed during pregnancy.

### 7.4 Suggested decision framework (synthesis, not a guideline)

| Scenario | Suggested approach |
|---|---|
| Asymptomatic niche | No treatment; counsel; early TVUS in future pregnancy |
| Symptomatic (spotting/pain), **no wish for pregnancy** | LNG-IUS or COC first; hysteroscopic resection if medical therapy fails or is declined |
| Symptomatic, **fertility wish**, RMT adequate | Hysteroscopic resection |
| Symptomatic or infertile, **thin RMT** (about <2–3 mm), large or branched niche | Laparoscopic/robotic (± hysteroscopic) repair |
| Infertility with intrauterine fluid and a niche, other causes excluded | Consider niche treatment before ART; the evidence for benefit is observational |
| Completed family, refractory symptoms | Hysterectomy |

### 7.5 Niche in pregnancy
- Perform TVUS at **6–7 weeks** to confirm placental location in women with prior CS, because CSP carries serious risk [16].
- A Delphi-based classification of CSP by the depth of sac herniation predicted management difficulty. In a small retrospective cohort, the subgroup with the sac extending beyond the uterine contour had repeated methotrexate courses and surgery in all cases [15].

## 8. Evidence gaps and research priorities

1. **Prevention.** The effect of single- versus double-layer closure is unresolved. Large, pragmatic RCTs should include standardized, contrast-enhanced imaging, and separate niche formation from symptoms. Purse-string closure needs confirmation in multicenter trials with long-term obstetric outcomes [8,9,11].
2. **Treatment comparisons.** The field lacks adequately powered RCTs comparing hysteroscopic resection, laparoscopic repair and LNG-IUS using CSDi criteria and patient-reported outcomes [12–14,22].
3. **Fertility.** Does treating a niche improve live birth after natural conception or ART? Current evidence is low-grade and observational [18,20].
4. **Outcome standardization.** Adopt the CSDi definition and a core outcome set for symptoms, imaging and obstetric outcomes [5].
5. **Prediction.** Which niches become symptomatic or dehisce in pregnancy? Candidate approaches include structured imaging features (RMT, branches, volume) and possibly AI-assisted ultrasound analysis (investigational).
6. **Microbiome and local inflammation.** Mechanistic studies of intrauterine fluid and microbial composition in women with niches [18].
7. **Obstetric safety.** Define the RMT threshold and surveillance schedule for planned vaginal birth after CS in women with a known niche.

---

## 9. Practical summary for clinicians

- Always differentiate the **sonographic niche** from **CSDi** and exclude other causes before attributing symptoms.
- Standardize TVUS reporting (length, depth, width, RMT, AMT, branches), ideally with contrast and after menses.
- Asymptomatic: reassure, counsel, early scan in future pregnancy.
- Symptomatic and not seeking pregnancy: **LNG-IUS** is a good first-line option based on current, limited data.
- Seeking pregnancy: **hysteroscopic resection** if RMT is adequate. **Laparoscopic/robotic repair** if RMT is thin.
- At CS: avoid very low hysterotomy and strangulating or locked sutures. Routine double-layer closure is not clearly beneficial. **Purse-string closure** is a promising technique pending confirmation.
